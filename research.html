IECL / Research vision

Connecting local resource use with environmental conservation and rehabilitation

Our goal is to advance understanding of soil–water–plant interactions and establish approaches that sustain local resource use while maintaining or restoring the environmental and productive functions of agricultural land and rural areas. Soil and water conservation is the foundation of this work.

We examine not only the properties of individual resources, but also how combinations and field conditions alter their functions and effectiveness. Linking basic experiments with field investigation, we seek measures that work under local conditions and can be sustained with available materials and maintenance.

Understand the processes

Examine water, sediment and solute movement together with plant and biological responses to investigate the processes behind observed changes.

Establish where measures work

Evaluate material and management effects in relation to soil, water quality, weather and field conditions.

Connect evidence with field practice

Consider environmental effects, resource recycling and maintenance together when assessing locally appropriate conservation and rehabilitation.

01 / THEMES

Soil and water conservation through soil–water–plant interactions

Water retention, infiltration and soil structure are linked to sediment and solute transport, plant roots, surface cover and transpiration. Biological activity and combinations of resources and materials can produce responses that cannot be explained by individual properties alone. We examine these properties and interactions through experiments and field investigation.

Materials and practices are evaluated in terms of the processes they affect, the conditions under which they work and their effects on other functions.

Soil erosion processes and conservation

Studies examine how soil structure, slope length and width, crop cover and ridge configuration influence detachment and transport by rainfall and surface runoff. Basic experiments and soil-loss measurements are linked with assessments of predictive equations.

Surface cover protects exposed soil, whereas incorporated materials may affect aggregation and permeability. Distinguishing these mechanisms helps assess erosion control under local conditions.

Water quality and soil–plant interactions

Research addresses watershed water quality, groundwater arsenic and plant-based water treatment, relating water chemistry to land use, soil and plants. Sampling and analysis in the upper Ayase River basin examined nonpoint-source pollution in rural areas.

Floating vetiver experiments found different responses in nitrogen, phosphorus and organic matter among water types. Treatment is assessed using multiple water-quality variables and the relevant operating conditions.

Soil degradation, desertification and local resource use

Research examines locally appropriate conservation and rehabilitation approaches to soil erosion, reduced water retention and salinization. Dryland studies have investigated water-retaining materials and pitcher irrigation as measures addressing land degradation associated with desertification. Plant residues, green manure and carbonized materials are also examined in soil management. Water retention, drainage, salt and nutrient movement and plant growth are evaluated together to understand how material properties and soil conditions affect performance.

Work on sugarcane residues in Okinawa, Sesbania green manure in Thailand and tsunami-affected farmland relates conservation and rehabilitation to local resources and degradation processes.

Plant growth and water use

Outdoor leaf-surface treatments and indoor LED irradiation are examined under their respective growing conditions. Plant morphology and transpiration are assessed together to investigate growth-related water demand and responses to treatments.

Reduced transpiration is not treated as beneficial on its own. Responses are interpreted in relation to plant growth, irradiation conditions and developmental stage.

Measurement and information tools supporting research

To evaluate soil, water and plant resources and their interactions, field investigation, experiments, analysis and measurement are complemented by GIS, remote sensing (RS), unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs, or drones) and IT applications. Methods are selected according to the research question, information requirements and data quality to support data collection, organisation and analysis. Previous studies have also used GIS and remote sensing for spatial assessment of land use and watersheds.

Models such as USLE and WEPP for soil erosion, and HYDRUS for soil water and solute transport, are used according to the research objective and evaluated alongside experimental and field measurements.

03 / DEVELOPMENT

From basic research to field investigation and technology development

Basic research on the mechanisms, prediction and control of water erosion underpins the programme. Faculty research and undergraduate and graduate research by former students have also addressed resource recycling, greening and soil and water measurement methods. Beyond the current undergraduate projects, this body of work provides a foundation for research addressing field problems as they arise.

Water erosion: mechanisms, prediction and control

Studies have examined how slope length and width, rainfall, crop cover, ridge configuration and soil structure influence soil loss. This work also considers rainfall characteristics in regions influenced by monsoon climates. Basic experiments on erosion processes and assessments of soil-loss equations provide evidence for conservation measures.

Rainfall types and soil-loss estimation (Kusaka, Fukada & Roy, 1994)
Crop cover and ridging factors (Roy, Kusaka & Fukada, 1995)
Soil structure and erodibility (Roy, Kohno & Shimada, 2008)

Crop residues for soil erosion control

To investigate locally appropriate approaches to red-soil runoff control in Okinawa, sugarcane residues were mixed with locally sampled Kunigami-Maji soil. Experiments examined changes in soil structure and permeability with treatment duration, evaluating the potential of local resources for soil improvement.

Related presentation (Roy, Uenishi & Yamamoto, 2006)
Related article (Roy, 2007)

Plant-derived soil flocculants

Research has examined plant extracts for promoting the flocculation and settling of soil particles suspended in runoff associated with soil erosion. Laboratory experiments using Okinawan red soil investigated differences among plant species and changes in water properties after extract addition.

Related presentation: Plant-derived soil flocculants and their effectiveness (Roy, 2009; PDF in Japanese)

Shortening composting time

Research on woody materials has examined wood vinegar and waste molasses as fermentation aids to shorten composting time, supporting the recycling of local organic resources.

Related article (Sato & Roy, 2010)

Low-cost rooftop greening units

Portable greening units using inexpensive and reused materials were constructed and assessed through soil moisture, temperature and plant growth measurements. The work examines affordable greening approaches, including water retention, drainage and maintenance.

Related presentation

A simple instrument for soil phase measurement

Research has also addressed the development of a simple instrument for determining the proportions of solids, water and air in soil, extending the laboratory’s work to measurement methods for soil assessment.

Subsurface drainage and soil water management

Studies have examined clay-pipe subsurface drainage and materials for improving soil permeability, relating water management to locally available materials and soil and water properties.

Related article (Kusaka, Fukada, Ohno & Roy, 1993)

Green manure for salt-affected soils in Thailand

Doctoral research examined the use of Sesbania rostrata as green manure in salt-affected soils in Thailand, investigating plant resources as part of agronomic approaches to soil management and crop production.
